Soft Gallery

About

Everyone finds a place for art in their life

Art is not hard to find any more. It is hard to trust. Scroll far enough and you will see ten thousand images and learn nothing about any of them — who made them, why, what they cost, whether the price is fair, what happens if it arrives and you were wrong about the wall.

Soft Gallery is built for the part that is actually difficult: the distance between seeing something and being sure about it. We keep the collection small, we put the artist's own account next to every work, and we tell you the price before you ask.

Curate

A small number of artists, chosen for the work rather than for their following. Every piece is selected by a person who has to defend the choice.

Explain

Provenance in the artist's own voice. How it was made, what it is about, what was going on at the time. Written and filmed in the studio.

Frame

Every work here hangs in the same frame, and it is our mark: a continuous bar with the lower-left corner left open. It carries the same teal-to-blue gradient as the mark, top to foot. Bar weight is what separates a historic work from a contemporary one.

Guide

A curator on the other end, for the first purchase or the fiftieth. Scale, framing, placement, and an honest answer when a work is wrong for you.

What we commit to

Prices are always shown. No "price on request". Artists keep 70%. Higher than the gallery standard, published rather than negotiated. Every work ships with provenance. Signed documentation of who made it, when and from what. Fourteen days to change your mind. Art has to live in the room before you know.

A note on this build

This is a demonstration and nothing in it is for sale. Sixteen works are hung fifty-nine times across five rooms, plus two sculptures, which is why you will meet the same picture in more than one room. That is deliberate — a work on limestone under a dark ceiling is not the same experience as the same work on white plaster.

Four are public domain and correctly attributed: Leonardo's Mona Lisa (Musée du Louvre), Monet's Woman with a Parasol — Madame Monet and Her Son (National Gallery of Art, Washington), David Roberts' The Alcázar, Segovia and Alfred Roll's The Wet Nurse. Their catalogue text is factual, though the Roll's date and holding collection are unverified.

Twelve are placeholders. Everything else here was supplied to test the layout. Their titles, artists, prices and provenance are invented, each is marked with an amber badge in the panel, and none may be published until authorship and reproduction rights have been established with the people who made them. One of the twelve — the Fauve portrait — appears to be an early twentieth-century painting whose author we have not identified; if it is by an artist who died after 1955 it is still in copyright.

The Press Room articles are placeholders, written to let the route be walked and judged. Replace the PRESS block with what Sasha and Nastia are actually publishing.

One work — Iris — carries a studio recording, as a test of whether the artist's own voice belongs on every piece. It has no transcript yet, which it needs before this is public.

Replace the IMAGES, VOICE and WORKS blocks at the top of the source with the real cohort and the gallery re-hangs itself — sizes, labels, spotlights and scale diagrams follow the data.
